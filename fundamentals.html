AML Fundamentals

What money laundering is, why it matters, and how the global fight against financial crime works.

The Basics

What is money laundering?

Money laundering is the process of making illegally obtained money — "dirty money" — appear legitimate. Criminals who earn income from drug trafficking, fraud, corruption, tax evasion, or other crimes need a way to use that money without drawing attention from law enforcement.

The term "money laundering" is believed to originate from the practice of organised crime groups in the United States using cash-intensive laundromats to mix illicit cash with legitimate business revenue — literally "laundering" the money through a legal business.

📌 Key Definition Money laundering is the process by which criminals disguise the original ownership and control of the proceeds of criminal conduct by making such proceeds appear to have been derived from a legitimate source.

Source: Financial Action Task Force (FATF)

According to the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC), the estimated amount of money laundered globally in one year is 2–5% of global GDP, or roughly $800 billion to $2 trillion. However, due to the clandestine nature of money laundering, precise statistics are impossible to determine.

Core Framework

The Three Stages of Money Laundering

Money laundering typically follows three stages. Not every scheme uses all three, and the stages may overlap — but this framework is foundational to understanding AML.

1️⃣

Stage 1: Placement

The first stage involves introducing illegal funds into the legitimate financial system. This is often the riskiest stage for criminals, as large amounts of cash are most conspicuous and most likely to attract attention.

Common methods include: depositing cash in small amounts below reporting thresholds (structuring/smurfing), purchasing monetary instruments (money orders, cashier's cheques), blending funds with cash-intensive businesses (restaurants, car washes), and physically smuggling currency across borders.

Note: This description is at the awareness level found in FATF and FinCEN public advisories. It describes patterns to help recognise suspicious activity, not to enable it.

2️⃣

Stage 2: Layering

Once placed in the financial system, launderers create complex layers of transactions to separate the money from its criminal origin. The goal is to make it difficult for authorities to trace the funds back to their source.

Common methods include: wire transfers between multiple accounts (especially across jurisdictions), converting cash into financial instruments, using shell companies and trusts to obscure ownership, and engaging in back-to-back lending arrangements.

3️⃣

Stage 3: Integration

In the final stage, the "cleaned" money is reintroduced into the legitimate economy. By this point, detection is extremely difficult because the money appears to come from legitimate sources.

Common methods include: investing in real estate, luxury goods, or legitimate businesses; creating false import/export invoices at inflated prices; and using front companies that lend money back to the launderer.

The Impact

Why AML matters

Money laundering is not a victimless crime. It enables and sustains the most serious criminal activities in the world. Without the ability to launder proceeds, most large-scale criminal enterprises would struggle to operate.

💊 Drug Trafficking

Drug cartels generate billions in cash that must be laundered to be usable.

💣 Terrorism Financing

Terrorist organisations use laundering networks to move and conceal funding.

🏛️ Corruption

Corrupt officials launder bribes and embezzled public funds through offshore structures.

👤 Human Trafficking

Trafficking networks launder profits to expand operations and evade detection.

Beyond enabling crime, money laundering undermines financial systems, distorts markets, and erodes trust in institutions. It can destabilise economies — particularly in developing countries — and diverts resources from productive use.

Key Distinctions

AML vs. CFT vs. Sanctions

These three terms are closely related but distinct. Understanding the difference is essential for anyone entering the compliance field.

AML Anti-Money Laundering

Laws, regulations, and procedures designed to prevent criminals from disguising illegally obtained funds as legitimate income. AML focuses on the proceeds of crime — the money flowing out from criminal activity.

CFT Counter-Financing of Terrorism

Measures to detect and prevent the financing of terrorist activities. Unlike AML, CFT deals with money flowing towards crime — the funds may even originate from legitimate sources (charitable donations, legal businesses) but are diverted to fund terrorism.

Sanctions Economic & Trade Sanctions

Restrictions imposed by governments and international bodies (UN, EU, OFAC) against specific countries, entities, or individuals. Sanctions compliance requires financial institutions to screen customers and transactions against sanctions lists and block prohibited dealings.

📌 Remember In practice, these three areas overlap significantly. Most compliance programs are structured as "AML/CFT" or "AML/Sanctions" functions. The FATF's Recommendations cover all three areas.
