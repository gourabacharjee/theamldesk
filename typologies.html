Typologies & Red Flags

Common money laundering patterns and warning signs — described at the same level of detail as public regulator advisories, for awareness purposes only.

⚠️ Content Notice This page describes money laundering typologies at the awareness level found in FATF, FinCEN, and other public regulator advisories. The purpose is to help compliance professionals, students, and the public recognise suspicious patterns — not to provide operational instructions. No information here goes beyond what is publicly available in official regulatory publications.

Typology

🏦 Structuring (Smurfing)

Structuring involves breaking large sums of money into smaller amounts that fall below regulatory reporting thresholds. Multiple individuals ("smurfs") may be used to make deposits at different branches or institutions.

Red Flags

  • Multiple cash deposits just below reporting thresholds (e.g., just under $10,000 in the US)
  • Deposits made at multiple branches or ATMs on the same day
  • Multiple individuals making deposits into the same account
  • Customer appears nervous or deliberately avoids interaction with staff

Source: FinCEN Advisory FIN-2014-A007; FATF Report on Money Laundering Typologies

Typology

📦 Trade-Based Money Laundering (TBML)

Trade-Based Money Laundering exploits the international trade system to move value across borders. Criminals manipulate trade invoices to disguise the transfer of money.

Common Techniques

  • Over-invoicing: Inflating the price of goods on import/export documents
  • Under-invoicing: Understating the value of goods shipped
  • Multiple invoicing: Issuing multiple invoices for the same shipment
  • Phantom shipments: Creating invoices for goods that don't exist

Red Flags

  • Significant discrepancies between the value of goods and their market price
  • Trade transactions with no apparent economic rationale
  • Commodities shipped to/from jurisdictions inconsistent with the buyer's or seller's business
  • Unusually complex trade structures involving multiple intermediaries

Source: FATF/Egmont Group Report on Trade-Based Money Laundering (2006, updated 2020)

Typology

🏢 Shell Companies & Corporate Structures

Shell companies are legal entities that have no significant assets or operations. While shell companies have legitimate uses (holding assets, M&A), criminals misuse them to obscure the origin and ownership of illicit funds.

How They're Misused

  • Creating layers of corporate ownership to hide the true beneficial owner
  • Moving funds through multiple shell company accounts across jurisdictions
  • Using nominee directors and shareholders to conceal control
  • Generating fake invoices between related shell companies to justify fund transfers

Red Flags

  • Companies incorporated in secrecy jurisdictions with minimal disclosure requirements
  • Complex multi-layered corporate structures with no clear business rationale
  • Nominee directors or shareholders with no apparent connection to the business
  • Company activities inconsistent with its stated purpose

Source: FATF Report on Misuse of Corporate Vehicles; Panama Papers ICIJ investigation (public domain)

Typology

₿ Virtual Assets & Crypto Mixing

Virtual assets (cryptocurrencies) present both opportunities and challenges for AML. While blockchain transactions are recorded on a public ledger, certain techniques can obscure the trail.

AML Concerns

  • Mixing/tumbling services: Pool multiple users' funds and redistribute them, breaking the chain of traceability
  • Privacy coins: Cryptocurrencies designed with enhanced privacy features (e.g., Monero, Zcash)
  • Peer-to-peer exchanges: Transactions without regulated intermediaries
  • Chain-hopping: Converting between different cryptocurrencies to break the audit trail

Red Flags

  • Transactions involving known mixing services or darknet marketplaces
  • Rapid conversion of crypto to fiat currency (or vice versa) without apparent business reason
  • Use of multiple wallets for no clear purpose
  • Customer unable or unwilling to explain the source of crypto assets

📌 FATF Travel Rule FATF Recommendation 16 (the "Travel Rule") requires Virtual Asset Service Providers (VASPs) to share originator and beneficiary information for virtual asset transfers — similar to wire transfer requirements for banks.

Source: FATF Updated Guidance on Virtual Assets and VASPs (2021); FinCEN Advisories

Typology

🏠 Real Estate

Real estate has long been identified as a vehicle for money laundering due to its ability to absorb large sums, appreciate in value, and provide a veneer of legitimacy.

Red Flags

  • All-cash purchases of high-value properties
  • Purchases through shell companies or trusts that conceal the true buyer
  • Property purchased significantly above or below market value
  • Rapid buying and selling ("flipping") without renovation or improvements
  • Use of third parties (lawyers, relatives) to purchase on behalf of the actual buyer

Source: FATF Report on Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing through the Real Estate Sector (2007); FinCEN Geographic Targeting Orders (GTOs)

Typology

💵 Cash-Intensive Businesses

Businesses that handle large volumes of cash (restaurants, car washes, retail stores, casinos) can be exploited to commingle illicit cash with legitimate revenue — a classic placement technique.

Red Flags

  • Revenue significantly exceeding what would be expected for the size/type of business
  • Business deposits large amounts of cash but has minimal operating expenses
  • Revenue patterns inconsistent with seasonal or market trends
  • New business immediately generating high cash revenue without apparent customer base

Source: FATF Guidance on AML/CFT-Related Data Collection; FinCEN/IRS public advisories
