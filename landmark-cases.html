Landmark AML Cases

Major enforcement actions and scandals that shaped modern AML compliance — summarised from public sources as lessons learned.

Case Study

HSBC — Mexico Drug Cartel Laundering (2012)

$1.9B Fine DPA — Deferred Prosecution Agreement 2012

What Happened

HSBC's US operations failed to maintain an adequate AML programme, allowing at least $881 million in drug trafficking proceeds — primarily from Mexican and Colombian drug cartels — to be laundered through the bank. The bank also violated US sanctions by processing transactions involving Cuba, Iran, Libya, Sudan, and Burma.

Key Failures

  • HSBC Mexico (HBMX) had the highest volume of US dollar purchases in Mexico but was classified as "standard" risk — not "high" risk
  • The bank's AML compliance department was understaffed and under-resourced
  • HSBC failed to monitor $60 trillion in wire transfers and $670 billion in wire transfers from Mexico
  • Bulk cash was physically transported across the border to HBMX branches in boxes specifically designed to fit through teller windows

Outcome

HSBC entered into a Deferred Prosecution Agreement (DPA) with the US Department of Justice, paying $1.92 billion in fines — at the time, the largest penalty ever imposed for BSA/AML violations. The bank was required to overhaul its compliance programme under a five-year monitorship.

Lessons Learned

Key Takeaway: An AML programme is only as strong as its weakest link. HSBC's case demonstrated that even the world's largest banks can fail spectacularly when compliance is under-resourced, when risk assessments are inaccurate, and when a "culture of compliance" is absent from senior management.

Sources: US Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations Report (2012); DOJ Press Release (Dec 11, 2012); FinCEN Assessment of Civil Money Penalty

Case Study

Danske Bank — Estonia Branch Scandal (2018)

~€200B Suspicious Flows Multiple Jurisdictions 2007–2015

What Happened

Danske Bank's Estonia branch processed approximately €200 billion in suspicious transactions from 2007 to 2015 — mostly from customers in Russia, Azerbaijan, and other former Soviet states. An internal investigation (the Bruun & Hjejle report) found that a significant portion of these transactions were suspicious.

Key Failures

  • The Estonia branch operated with significant autonomy and insufficient oversight from headquarters
  • Non-resident customers (primarily from Russia/CIS) made up a disproportionate share of profits
  • Internal whistleblower warnings were ignored for years
  • KYC procedures were grossly inadequate — many customer files were essentially empty
  • The bank's governance structure failed to escalate red flags to the board

Outcome

Danske Bank's CEO resigned. The bank was fined by multiple regulators. In December 2022, Danske Bank pleaded guilty in the US and agreed to forfeit $2 billion. The case led to significant regulatory reforms across the EU and contributed to the creation of the EU's Anti-Money Laundering Authority (AMLA).

Lessons Learned

Key Takeaway: Branch-level AML controls must be integrated with group-level governance. Whistleblower reports must be taken seriously. The Danske Bank case catalysed the EU's move toward a single AML supervisor (AMLA) and highlighted the danger of treating high-risk branches as profit centres without adequate controls.

Sources: Bruun & Hjejle Report (2018); Danish FSA Report; DOJ Press Release (Dec 13, 2022)

Case Study

1MDB — Malaysia Development Fund Scandal

$4.5B+ Misappropriated Multi-Country 2009–2015

What Happened

1Malaysia Development Berhad (1MDB) was a Malaysian state investment fund established to promote economic development. Between 2009 and 2015, at least $4.5 billion was misappropriated from the fund by high-ranking officials and their associates, in what the US Department of Justice described as the "largest kleptocracy case" in its history.

How the Money Was Laundered

  • Funds were diverted through a web of shell companies in the British Virgin Islands, Seychelles, and other jurisdictions
  • Multiple international banks facilitated the transfers, often without adequate due diligence
  • Proceeds were used to purchase luxury real estate (New York, Beverly Hills, London), a superyacht, artwork (including a Van Gogh and a Monet), and to finance the Hollywood film The Wolf of Wall Street
  • Funds were routed through accounts at Goldman Sachs, Deutsche Bank, JPMorgan, and others

Outcome

Former Malaysian Prime Minister Najib Razak was convicted of corruption and money laundering (2020). Goldman Sachs agreed to pay over $5 billion globally in settlements. Multiple individuals were charged across several jurisdictions. The DOJ recovered over $1.7 billion in assets.

Lessons Learned

Key Takeaway: The 1MDB case demonstrated how PEPs (Politically Exposed Persons) can exploit their positions to embezzle massive sums, and how failures in correspondent banking due diligence and beneficial ownership verification enable cross-border laundering at scale. It underscored the critical importance of EDD for PEPs and state-owned entities.

Sources: US DOJ Civil Asset Forfeiture Complaints (2016–2019); Malaysian Court Judgments; Goldman Sachs DPA (2020)

Case Study

FTX — Cryptocurrency Exchange Collapse (2022)

$8B+ Customer Funds Lost Crypto/Fintech 2022

What Happened

FTX, one of the world's largest cryptocurrency exchanges, collapsed in November 2022 after it was revealed that customer funds had been secretly transferred to Alameda Research, a trading firm controlled by FTX founder Sam Bankman-Fried. An estimated $8 billion in customer deposits was missing.

AML/Compliance Failures

  • FTX had virtually no compliance department — it reportedly had no Chief Compliance Officer for significant periods
  • Customer funds were commingled with proprietary trading funds without proper controls
  • Corporate governance was essentially non-existent — decisions were made by a small group with no board oversight
  • FTX operated in multiple jurisdictions with minimal regulatory licensing or compliance infrastructure
  • The bankruptcy examiner found a "complete failure of corporate controls"

Outcome

Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted on seven criminal counts including fraud and money laundering conspiracy (November 2023) and sentenced to 25 years in prison. The FTX bankruptcy estate has been recovering assets for creditors. The case accelerated global regulatory efforts to bring crypto exchanges under AML/CFT frameworks.

Lessons Learned

Key Takeaway: FTX demonstrated that compliance is not optional — even in the fast-moving crypto industry. The absence of basic governance, segregation of customer funds, and AML controls created an environment where massive fraud could occur unchecked. The case has been a catalyst for crypto regulation worldwide, including the EU's MiCA (Markets in Crypto-Assets) regulation.

Sources: US DOJ Indictment (Dec 2022); SEC Complaint; FTX Bankruptcy Court Filings; Sentencing Memorandum (2024)
