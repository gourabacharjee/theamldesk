Global Regulatory Landscape

How nations and international bodies fight money laundering — the key laws, standards, and frameworks you need to know.

Financial Action Task Force (FATF)

The FATF is an intergovernmental organisation founded in 1989 by the G7. It is the global standard-setter for AML/CFT policies. Its 40 Recommendations form the basis for virtually every national AML law in the world.

Key Facts

  • Members: 39 member jurisdictions and 2 regional organisations
  • FATF-Style Regional Bodies (FSRBs): 9 regional bodies extending coverage to 200+ jurisdictions
  • Mutual Evaluations: Peer reviews assessing each country's AML/CFT effectiveness
  • Grey List/Black List: Countries with strategic deficiencies in their AML frameworks

The 40 Recommendations (Grouped)

  • Recommendations 1–2: Assessing risks and applying a risk-based approach
  • Recommendations 3–4: Money laundering offence and confiscation
  • Recommendations 5–8: Terrorist financing and proliferation financing
  • Recommendations 9–23: Preventive measures (CDD, record-keeping, reporting, etc.)
  • Recommendations 24–25: Transparency of legal persons and arrangements
  • Recommendations 26–35: Powers and responsibilities of authorities
  • Recommendations 36–40: International cooperation

📌 Why FATF matters for your career Every AML interview will expect you to know what FATF is, what the 40 Recommendations cover, and what the Grey List/Black List mean. FATF is the foundation of global AML policy.

Official source: FATF Recommendations (fatf-gafi.org) ↗

EU Anti-Money Laundering Directives

The European Union has issued a series of AML Directives (AMLDs) that member states must transpose into national law. Each directive has progressively strengthened AML requirements.

Evolution of EU AMLDs

1991 — 1st AMLD

First Anti-Money Laundering Directive

Applied only to the financial sector; focused on drug-related money laundering.

2001 — 2nd AMLD

Extended Scope

Expanded predicate offences beyond drug crimes; included lawyers, accountants, real estate agents.

2005 — 3rd AMLD

Risk-Based Approach

Introduced the risk-based approach to CDD; aligned with FATF 2003 Recommendations.

2015 — 4th AMLD

Beneficial Ownership

Required member states to maintain central registers of beneficial ownership; strengthened CDD and PEP provisions.

2018 — 5th AMLD

Crypto & Transparency

Extended AML rules to virtual currency exchanges and custodian wallet providers; made beneficial ownership registers publicly accessible.

2024 — 6th AMLD / AML Package

EU-wide AML Authority (AMLA)

Established the Anti-Money Laundering Authority (AMLA) based in Frankfurt; introduced a directly applicable AML Regulation (AMLR); harmonised rules across all member states.

Official source: European Commission AML/CFT page ↗

United States: BSA, Patriot Act & FinCEN

Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) — 1970

The Bank Secrecy Act (also called the Currency and Foreign Transactions Reporting Act) is the foundation of US AML law. It requires financial institutions to:

  • File Currency Transaction Reports (CTRs) for transactions exceeding $10,000
  • File Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) for suspicious transactions
  • Maintain records of certain transactions
  • Implement AML compliance programs

USA PATRIOT Act — 2001

Enacted after the September 11 attacks, the Uniting and Strengthening America by Providing Appropriate Tools Required to Intercept and Obstruct Terrorism (USA PATRIOT) Act significantly expanded AML requirements:

  • Section 311: Special measures for jurisdictions, institutions, or transactions of "primary money laundering concern"
  • Section 312: Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD) for correspondent banking and private banking
  • Section 314: Information sharing between government and financial institutions
  • Section 326: Customer Identification Program (CIP) requirements

FinCEN — Financial Crimes Enforcement Network

FinCEN is the US bureau responsible for administering the BSA. It collects and analyses financial data to combat money laundering, terrorist financing, and other financial crimes.

Corporate Transparency Act (CTA) — 2021

Requires companies to report beneficial ownership information to FinCEN, addressing the misuse of anonymous shell companies.

Official source: FinCEN (fincen.gov) ↗

India: PMLA 2002 & RBI Guidelines

Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA) — 2002

India's primary AML legislation, the PMLA, came into force in 2005. It criminalises money laundering, provides for confiscation of property derived from laundering, and establishes the framework for India's AML regime.

  • Scheduled Offences: The PMLA lists "scheduled offences" (predicate offences) in its Schedule — including drug trafficking, corruption, fraud, terrorism, and many others
  • Punishment: 3–7 years imprisonment and fine; up to 10 years for offences under the NDPS Act
  • Enforcement Directorate (ED): The agency responsible for investigating and enforcing PMLA cases
  • Adjudicating Authority: Confirms provisional attachment orders for properties involved in money laundering

Financial Intelligence Unit — India (FIU-IND)

India's national agency for receiving, processing, analysing, and disseminating information related to suspected financial transactions. Reporting entities file Cash Transaction Reports (CTRs) and Suspicious Transaction Reports (STRs) with FIU-IND.

RBI KYC Master Direction

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) issues detailed KYC/AML/CFT directions under the Master Direction on KYC (updated periodically). All banks and regulated financial institutions in India must comply with these guidelines.

Official sources: FIU-IND ↗ | RBI ↗

United Kingdom: POCA & MLR

Proceeds of Crime Act (POCA) — 2002

POCA is the UK's primary legislation for combating money laundering. It creates three main money laundering offences:

  • Section 327: Concealing, disguising, converting, transferring, or removing criminal property
  • Section 328: Entering into or becoming concerned in an arrangement facilitating the use of criminal property
  • Section 329: Acquiring, using, or possessing criminal property

POCA also established the "all crimes" approach — meaning any crime generating proceeds can be a predicate offence for money laundering (no minimum threshold).

Money Laundering Regulations (MLR) — 2017

The MLR 2017 (amended 2019, 2022) implements the EU's 4th and 5th AML Directives into UK law. They apply to the "regulated sector" and require:

  • Customer Due Diligence (CDD) including identification and verification
  • Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD) for high-risk situations
  • Ongoing monitoring of business relationships
  • Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) to the National Crime Agency (NCA)
  • Appointment of a Money Laundering Reporting Officer (MLRO)

Key UK Bodies

  • NCA (National Crime Agency): Receives and processes SARs via the UKFIU
  • FCA (Financial Conduct Authority): Supervises AML compliance for financial firms
  • HMRC: AML supervisor for accountants, estate agents, and certain other sectors

Official source: NCA (nationalcrimeagency.gov.uk) ↗

Wolfsberg Principles

The Wolfsberg Group is an association of 13 global banks that develops frameworks and guidance for managing financial crime risks. Founded in 2000, it publishes voluntary principles that serve as industry best practices.

Member Banks

Barclays, Bank of America, Citigroup, Deutsche Bank, Goldman Sachs, HSBC, JP Morgan Chase, MUFG, Société Générale, Standard Chartered, Credit Suisse (now UBS), and UBS.

Key Wolfsberg Publications

  • AML Principles for Private Banking (2012) — CDD standards for private banking clients
  • AML Principles for Correspondent Banking (2014) — Managing risks in correspondent banking relationships
  • Guidance on Beneficial Ownership (2012) — Standards for identifying beneficial owners
  • Transaction Screening Guidance (2019) — Best practices for sanctions and watch-list screening
  • Payment Transparency Standards (2017) — Ensuring complete originator/beneficiary information

📌 Note While the Wolfsberg Principles are voluntary (not legally binding), they are widely adopted by major financial institutions and often referenced by regulators as good practice.

Official source: Wolfsberg Group (wolfsberg-principles.com) ↗
