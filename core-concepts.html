Core AML Concepts

The essential compliance concepts every AML professional must understand — explained in plain English.

Identity Verification

KYC — Know Your Customer

Know Your Customer (KYC) is the process by which financial institutions verify the identity of their clients. It is the cornerstone of every AML programme — you cannot assess risk if you don't know who your customer is.

KYC is not a one-time event. It's an ongoing obligation that begins at account opening and continues throughout the relationship, with periodic reviews based on the customer's risk level.

KYC Typically Involves

  • Customer Identification Programme (CIP): Collecting and verifying identifying information (name, date of birth, address, ID documents)
  • Customer Due Diligence (CDD): Understanding the customer's business, purpose of the relationship, expected transaction patterns
  • Ongoing Monitoring: Continuously reviewing transactions and updating customer information
Risk-Based Approach

CDD & EDD — Due Diligence Levels

Due diligence is not one-size-fits-all. The depth of scrutiny should match the level of risk. This is the risk-based approach — the foundational principle of modern AML regulation.

📋

Simplified DD (SDD)

Applied when the risk of ML/TF is low. Reduced verification requirements — for example, publicly listed companies with transparent ownership.

📝

Standard CDD

The baseline level applied to all customers. Identify the customer, verify identity, understand the purpose and nature of the business relationship.

🔬

Enhanced DD (EDD)

Applied to high-risk customers. Additional verification, senior management approval, source of wealth/funds investigation, more frequent monitoring.

When is EDD Required?

  • Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs) and their family/associates
  • Customers from high-risk jurisdictions (FATF Grey/Black List countries)
  • Correspondent banking relationships
  • Complex or unusual transactions with no apparent economic purpose
  • Non-face-to-face business relationships
  • Private banking clients
High-Risk Customers

PEPs — Politically Exposed Persons

A Politically Exposed Person (PEP) is an individual who holds or has held a prominent public function. PEPs are considered high-risk because their position may make them vulnerable to corruption and bribery.

Types of PEPs

  • Foreign PEPs: Hold prominent public functions in a foreign country (always high risk)
  • Domestic PEPs: Hold prominent public functions in the home country (risk-based assessment)
  • International Organisation PEPs: Senior management of international organisations (UN, World Bank, etc.)

PEP Family & Close Associates

AML regulations also extend PEP scrutiny to family members (spouse, children, parents) and close associates (known business partners, joint beneficial owners).

⚠️ Important Being a PEP is not criminal — it simply means enhanced due diligence is required. PEP status does not imply guilt or wrongdoing.

Transparency

Beneficial Ownership

The beneficial owner is the natural person who ultimately owns or controls a customer entity, or on whose behalf a transaction is conducted. Identifying beneficial owners is critical because criminals often hide behind shell companies, trusts, and nominee arrangements.

FATF Definition

FATF Recommendation 10 defines beneficial owner as: the natural person(s) who ultimately owns or controls a customer and/or the natural person on whose behalf a transaction is being conducted. Typical thresholds are 25% ownership/control (varies by jurisdiction).

Why It Matters

Shell companies with hidden ownership have been at the centre of major money laundering scandals (1MDB, Danske Bank). Regulators globally are now requiring central beneficial ownership registers and stricter identification procedures.

Prohibited Parties

Sanctions Screening

Sanctions screening is the process of checking customers, transactions, and counterparties against official sanctions lists to ensure the institution is not dealing with prohibited parties.

Key Sanctions Lists

  • UN Security Council Consolidated List
  • OFAC SDN List (US — Specially Designated Nationals)
  • EU Consolidated Sanctions List
  • UK HM Treasury Sanctions List
  • India MHA/MEA Sanctions Lists

Types of Screening

  • Customer screening: At onboarding and periodically (against updated lists)
  • Transaction screening: Real-time screening of payment messages (originator, beneficiary, intermediaries)
  • Name screening: Matching against PEP lists, adverse media, and enforcement databases
Detection

Transaction Monitoring

Transaction monitoring is the ongoing process of reviewing customer transactions to identify unusual or suspicious patterns that may indicate money laundering, terrorist financing, or other financial crimes.

How It Works

  • Rule-based systems: Predefined scenarios/thresholds that trigger alerts (e.g., transactions above a certain amount, rapid movement of funds, transactions with high-risk countries)
  • Behavioural analytics: AI/ML-based systems that build customer profiles and flag deviations from expected behaviour
  • Alert investigation: Compliance analysts review alerts, investigate, and determine whether to file a SAR/STR or close as false positive

📌 Career Tip Transaction monitoring analyst is one of the most common entry-level roles in AML compliance. Understanding alert investigation workflows is essential for fresher interviews.

Reporting

SAR/STR Filing

When a financial institution identifies a suspicious transaction or activity, it is legally required to file a report with the appropriate Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU).

SAR

Suspicious Activity Report — used in the US (filed with FinCEN), UK (filed with NCA), and other jurisdictions.

STR

Suspicious Transaction Report — used in India (filed with FIU-IND), Australia, and FATF terminology.

Key Principles

  • Tipping off: It is a criminal offence to inform the customer that a SAR/STR has been filed about them
  • Safe harbour: Institutions and individuals are protected from liability for filing SARs/STRs in good faith
  • Timeliness: Reports must be filed within prescribed deadlines (varies by jurisdiction)
  • Quality: Reports should contain sufficient detail for the FIU to assess and take action
Governance

Three Lines of Defence

The Three Lines of Defence model is the standard governance framework for managing compliance risk in financial institutions. It defines who is responsible for what.

1st Line
Business Units

Own the risk. Front office, relationship managers, operations.

→
2nd Line
Compliance

Oversee and advise. AML/compliance team, risk management.

→
3rd Line
Internal Audit

Independently assure. Reviews effectiveness of 1st and 2nd lines.

📌 Interview Favourite The Three Lines of Defence model is one of the most commonly asked questions in AML/compliance interviews. Be prepared to explain each line and give examples.
